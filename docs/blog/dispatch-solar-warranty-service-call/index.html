Dispatch a Solar Warranty Service Call (and Who Pays)

By Rodney Lunt · 9 November 2026 · 13 min read

Share:

The phone rings on a Tuesday morning. A customer says their system “isn’t working” and they want someone out today. Someone in the office books the nearest subbie, the subbie drives forty minutes, finds an inverter fault that needs a manufacturer replacement, and invoices you for the call-out. Then nobody can agree who pays for it.

I’ve watched this play out in install businesses for twenty years. The fault itself is rarely the expensive part. What costs you is sending someone out before you know what you’re dealing with, then arguing about the bill afterwards with no record of who agreed to what.

This article is the dispatch workflow I’d want every install business to run: triage, classify the fault, brief the subbie properly, close the loop, and know who pays at each step. It is general information, not legal advice. Who actually pays depends on your contracts, so get a lawyer to look at yours.

Why warranty call-outs bleed money

Most install businesses treat a warranty call like a new job: book it, send someone, sort out the money later. That’s backwards. A new job has a quote and a signed contract before anyone gets in the van. A warranty call usually has neither.

The guidance online doesn’t help much, because each piece covers only one part of the problem. Legal guides cover contract clauses, field-ops guides cover triage, and liability guides cover who’s responsible. Very few put the whole thing into one sequence you can hand to your office staff.

The sequence that works is the one the Zestmate field-ops guide uses for commercial callbacks: triage, dispatch, close (Zestmate, n.d.). It fits residential solar just as well. I’d only add one step between triage and dispatch: classify the fault and decide who’s paying before anyone moves.

Step 1: Triage before anyone gets in a van

The first call is where you save or lose the money. Whoever answers the phone needs a script, and it should capture the following:

  • The symptom in the customer’s words. “No power from the panels”, “inverter beeping”, “app shows zero”, “water coming through the ceiling”.
  • Photos. The inverter screen, the switchboard, anything visible on the roof from the ground, any water damage.
  • The inverter fault code. Most customers can read it off the screen if you ask. That one code often tells you more than the rest of the call.
  • Monitoring data. If you have portal access, check generation history before you hang up. A sudden drop to zero reads very differently from a slow decline.
  • Install date and warranty status. Pull up the job record: who installed it, when, what equipment went in, and what warranties apply.

Ask about safety first, too. Burning smells, scorch marks, water near electrical gear or anything sparking is a different track. Tell the customer to isolate the system if it’s safe to do so, and get someone qualified out without waiting on any cost discussion. If you run an after-hours roster for exactly this, I’ve written about on-call pay for solar service techs.

For everything else, some faults can be fixed over the phone. An inverter that tripped after a grid event, a monitoring dongle that dropped off Wi-Fi, an isolator someone switched off: talking the customer through it is cheaper than a truck roll.

Step 2: Classify the fault before you dispatch

Once you’ve triaged, put the fault in one of three buckets. This decides who pays, so do it on paper, not in your head.

Workmanship defect. Something about how the system was installed: a loose termination, a poorly sealed roof penetration, an isolator that’s taken on water because of how it was mounted, cabling that wasn’t secured. This falls under your workmanship warranty, and if a subbie did the work, possibly back onto them.

Product or performance fault. The equipment itself has failed or underperforms: an inverter that’s died, a panel with a manufacturing defect, an optimiser that’s given up. This falls under the manufacturer’s product or performance warranty. Manufacturers generally run their own claim processes and evidence requirements. Sungrow’s Australian procedure, for example, sets out how claims are lodged and what information is needed (Sungrow Australia, n.d.).

Customer-caused or external. Someone else’s tradie drilled through a cable, the customer turned off the system and forgot, a storm damaged the array, the grid voltage is out of range, or shading from a new extension has killed output. Generally not your warranty and not the manufacturer’s.

The split between installer workmanship warranties and manufacturer product warranties is well established (energy.gov.au, n.d.), and manufacturers themselves describe responsibility in similar terms (Fronius, 2026).

There’s a third layer sitting over both: the Australian Consumer Law. The consumer guarantees apply regardless of what your written warranty says, and the customer’s remedies (repair, replacement, refund) come through the business that sold them the system (ACCC, n.d.). In plain terms, the customer comes to you. You can’t send them off to chase the inverter brand on their own and call it done. What you recover from the manufacturer or subbie is a separate conversation that happens behind the scenes.

If you can’t classify the fault from triage, say so. “Unknown, needs diagnosis” is a legitimate category, and it changes how you dispatch.

Step 3: Work out who pays the call-out

Here’s the table I’d want on the wall of every office that books service calls. It’s a starting point, not a legal ruling: your customer contract, your subcontractor agreement and the manufacturer’s warranty terms decide the real answer.

ScenarioWho usually carries the call-out costWhat to check
Workmanship defect, your own crewYouYour workmanship warranty terms and period
Workmanship defect, subbie installed itYou, then recovered from the subbieYour subcontractor agreement’s defect and rectification clause
Manufacturer product faultThe part is usually covered; labour and call-out may not beThe manufacturer’s warranty: labour allowance, call-out cover, freight, removal and refit
Customer-caused or third-party damageThe customer, or their insurerThat you told them about the charge before dispatch
No fault foundDepends on what was agreed beforehandWhether the customer accepted a diagnostic fee in writing
Unknown at triageAgree in advance who pays under each outcomeGet written acceptance before dispatch

The product-fault row catches more businesses than any other. A manufacturer replacing a failed inverter is one thing. Paying your subbie to drive out, remove the old unit, fit the new one and recommission it is something else, and manufacturer labour allowances often don’t cover all of it. Read the warranty terms for the brands you install before the first claim, not during it. I’ve covered how to vet a supplier’s warranty process before you commit to a brand.

The no-fault-found row is where most arguments start. If you think there’s a real chance the visit finds nothing wrong with your work or the gear, tell the customer before you dispatch: “If we find a fault covered by warranty, there’s no charge. If it’s not a warranty issue, there’s a call-out fee of X.” Get a yes in writing, even if it’s a text reply.

Liability guidance you’ll find online is often US-oriented. The SurgePV post on installer liability in warranty claims makes useful points about documentation and the installer’s exposure (SurgePV, n.d.), but Australian rules differ, especially around the consumer guarantees. Take the operational lessons, not the legal conclusions.

Step 4: Back-charging the subcontractor

If the fault is workmanship and your subbie did the install, you’ll want to recover the cost. Whether you can depends almost entirely on what your subcontractor agreement says.

Sprintlaw’s guide to solar installer subcontractor agreements covers the clauses that matter, including defects, warranties and liability between you and the subbie (Sprintlaw, n.d.). The gap I see most often is that the agreement mentions defects in vague terms but says nothing about call-outs, response times or what happens if the subbie won’t come back.

Here’s the kind of wording I’d want a lawyer to start from. It is a sample only and needs proper review for your business and state:

Defect rectification and call-outs. If a defect arises from work performed by the Subcontractor, the Subcontractor must attend and rectify the defect at its own cost, including labour, travel and materials, within [X] business days of written notice (or [Y] hours for safety-related defects). If the Subcontractor fails to attend within that time, the Contractor may engage another person to rectify the defect and may recover the reasonable cost from the Subcontractor, including by set-off against amounts owed.

Three things make a clause like that work in practice. A clear trigger (written notice), a clear deadline, and a clear fallback if the subbie doesn’t turn up. Without the fallback, you’re stuck waiting while the customer gets angrier.

Evidence decides whether you actually recover the cost. I’ve gone into the full process in how to back-charge a solar subcontractor, but the short version is this: original install photos, the triage record, and photos from the rectification visit showing what was wrong.

Step 5: Brief the subbie properly

A bad brief turns a one-visit fix into two visits. “Go to 14 Smith St, inverter’s playing up” is not a brief. Here’s the dispatch checklist I’d send with every warranty call:

  1. Job address and access. Gate codes, dogs, roof access notes, where the switchboard and inverter are.
  2. Customer contact. Name, best number, and availability window.
  3. Fault description. The symptom, the fault code, and what the monitoring shows.
  4. Photos. Everything the customer sent, plus the original install photos.
  5. Equipment details. Inverter and panel make, model and serial numbers, so they can bring the right parts.
  6. Warranty basis and who’s paying. Workmanship (theirs or yours), manufacturer claim, or customer-paid. The subbie needs to know if this visit is on their dime.
  7. Accreditation and insurance check. Confirm their solar installer accreditation is current and you hold an up-to-date certificate of currency for their insurance. Check it in the office before dispatch, not afterwards. My piece on public liability insurance for accredited installers covers what to look for.
  8. Expected response time. When you’ve told the customer someone will be there, and when you need a report back.
  9. Evidence required on site. Before and after photos, fault code screenshots, serial numbers of any removed and replacement parts, and test results.

If the job is a manufacturer claim, add the manufacturer’s evidence requirements to the brief. Getting the subbie to capture the right photos and serials on the first visit saves you a second trip just to satisfy an RMA form.

Step 6: Close the loop

The job isn’t finished when the subbie leaves site. It’s finished when the paperwork is.

  • Photo evidence received. Before and after, attached to the job record, not sitting in a subbie’s camera roll. A job photo capture app helps here.
  • Customer sign-off. A short confirmation that the system is working and the visit is complete.
  • Cost allocation recorded. Who paid for the call-out, the labour and the parts, and on what basis.
  • Warranty log updated. Fault type, equipment, install date, outcome. Over time this shows you which brands, which subbies and which install practices generate callbacks.
  • Costs recovered. Lodge the manufacturer claim, issue the back-charge to the subbie, or invoice the customer, and diary a follow-up so it doesn’t get forgotten.

That warranty log is the bit almost nobody keeps, and it’s the most valuable. When you can see that one subbie’s installs generate three times the callbacks, or that one inverter model keeps failing, you’ve got the data to make a real decision. Without it you’re running on gut feel and whoever complained most recently. If callbacks are already piling up, my article on defect rectification job management goes deeper on the workflow.

The common mistakes

These are the failures I see over and over:

Dispatching before checking the warranty basis. The subbie’s on site before anyone has looked at the install date or what’s covered. Now you’re paying for a visit you might have been able to charge to someone else.

Vague call-out clauses. The subbie agreement says they’ll “fix defects” but nothing about cost, response time or what happens if they don’t. Every back-charge becomes a negotiation.

No response-time expectations. The customer was told “this week”, the subbie heard “when you can”. The customer rings back angry on Friday.

No record of who agreed to pay. The customer said yes to a diagnostic fee on the phone, then disputes the invoice. The subbie agreed to cover the call-out in a phone call, then forgets. If it isn’t written down, it didn’t happen.

Treating the manufacturer claim as the customer’s problem. Under the consumer guarantees, the customer’s remedy runs through you. Sending them off to deal with the brand themselves is a fast way to a complaint.

FAQ

Who pays the call-out fee when a solar inverter fails under warranty?

It depends on the manufacturer’s warranty terms and your contracts. The replacement unit is usually covered, but the manufacturer’s labour allowance often doesn’t cover the full cost of the call-out, removal and refit. Check each brand’s terms before you need them, and remember the customer’s consumer guarantee remedies run through you.

Can I charge a customer for a call-out if no fault is found?

Generally only if you told them about the charge before dispatch and they agreed. Make the terms clear on the phone (“no charge if it’s a warranty fault, a call-out fee if it isn’t”) and get written acceptance, even a text reply.

Can I make a subcontractor pay to fix their own faulty work?

Yes, if your subcontractor agreement says so and you can prove the defect came from their work. The clause should cover cost, response time and your right to engage someone else and recover the cost if they don’t attend. Have a lawyer review the wording.

What should I check before sending a subbie to a warranty job?

Confirm the install date, equipment and warranty basis, classify the fault, agree who’s paying, and check the subbie’s accreditation and certificate of currency are current. Then send a full brief with photos, fault codes, serial numbers and the evidence you need captured on site.

Do I have to handle the manufacturer warranty claim for the customer?

In practice, yes. Under the Australian Consumer Law the customer can seek a remedy from the business that sold them the system. You deal with the customer, then pursue the manufacturer behind the scenes.
