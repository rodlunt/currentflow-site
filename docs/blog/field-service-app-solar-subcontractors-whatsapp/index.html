Best Field Service App for Solar Subbies: Replace WhatsApp

By Rodney Lunt · 20 October 2026 · 13 min read

Share:

Most solar install businesses I know have the same setup. They don’t have a “system”. They have a WhatsApp group with the boss, the office, three crews and a subbie who left eight months ago and still gets every message.

Job addresses go in there. So do switchboard photos, roof shots, serial number labels, “running late” texts, a photo of someone’s dog, and a voice note about which inverter is on the ute. It works fine until somebody needs to find something. Then it stops working completely.

I’ve spent twenty years in and around this trade: sales, supply, admin, certificate trading, and building quoting tools and CRM processes from scratch. I’ve seen this pattern in more businesses than I can count. This article is written for the operator who knows the chat group has become the job record, and wants a way out that the crews will actually use.

Why WhatsApp groups fail solar jobs

WhatsApp is a great messaging app. It was never built to be a job file, and solar jobs need a job file more than most trades do.

Photos get buried, compressed or lost. By default, chat apps tend to compress images when they’re sent. A serial number label that was readable on the installer’s phone can turn into a blur by the time it reaches the office. Even when the photo is fine, it’s sitting in a thread of hundreds of messages covering a dozen jobs. Finding the panel serials for one address three months later means scrolling, guessing and hoping.

There’s no audit trail. A chat thread can tell you roughly when a message was sent. It doesn’t reliably tell you which job a photo belongs to, who took it, whether it was taken on site, or whether someone deleted or replaced it. When evidence is needed later, “it’s somewhere in the group” isn’t evidence.

Personal and work phones get mixed. The photos live on the subbie’s personal phone, often backed up to their personal cloud. Customer homes, faces and addresses end up next to their family photos. I covered the privacy side of this in detail in my BYOD policy for solar subcontractors, and it’s a bigger exposure than most owners realise.

Nobody owns the job. In a group chat, a question like “who’s picking up the battery for Tuesday?” gets three thumbs-up emojis and no answer. Ownership is implied, never assigned. That’s how two crews turn up to one job and nobody turns up to the other.

When the subbie leaves, the record leaves too. If your evidence lives on their phone and in a group they can exit, it walks out the door with them.

Why this matters more in solar than in plumbing

Here’s the part that makes it more than an annoyance.

Small-scale technology certificates (STCs) are created from an eligible system’s deemed generation. They’re not a rebate. They’re tradeable certificates that liable entities buy and surrender under the Renewable Energy Target, which is why I’ll always push back when people call them one (more on that in STCs are not a rebate). The Clean Energy Regulator (n.d.-a) sets out the process for creating them, and the documentation behind each claim has to stand up if the regulator comes asking.

The CER has also published a solar battery photo guide (Clean Energy Regulator, n.d.-b) setting out the photos expected for battery installations. Separately, the Clean Energy Council’s advice on documentation (Clean Energy Council, n.d.) covers the paperwork accredited installers are expected to keep.

Several vendor blogs go further. Some claim, for example, that geotagged and timestamped photos became mandatory from 1 March 2026 and that the regulator now uses automated checks on photos. Those posts are selling photo tools, so read the CER’s own documents before you rely on that. Whatever the exact rule, the direction is obvious: the regulator wants photos it can trust, tied to a specific install, captured on site.

A chat group fails every one of those tests. And if a photo problem holds up a claim, that’s cash sitting in limbo, the same squeeze I wrote about in the STC lodgement cashflow gap.

Why the “best field service app” lists don’t help

If you search for the best field service app right now, you’ll get the usual listicles. TechWire Global (n.d.) runs through field service management (FSM) apps for field teams. ServiceTitan (n.d.) lists sixteen mobile apps and how to choose. ProField (n.d.) lists twenty FSM tools.

They’re useful as a broad map. But two of the three are published by software vendors, so treat the rankings with that in mind. And all of them compare the same things: scheduling, dispatch, invoicing and price, for plumbing, HVAC and electrical service businesses.

None of them talk about:

  • Subcontractors who aren’t your employees and won’t carry a company phone
  • STC and CER photo evidence
  • Installer accreditation records
  • A sales rep handing a signed job to a crew they’ve never met
  • How to get people off a chat habit that’s been running for years

A plumber doing a callout cares about dispatch and getting paid on the spot. A solar crew cares about whether the photos, serials and sign-off for that install are captured properly, because someone will need them months or years later.

The must-have checklist for solar field work

Forget the 40-row feature comparison. If an app can’t do these things well, it won’t replace the chat group, no matter what else it does.

1. Photos attached to the job, not to a conversation. Every photo should land against a specific job and address the moment it’s taken. No forwarding, no “I’ll send them tonight”.

2. Timestamps and capture data you can rely on. At minimum you want the time the photo was taken, who took it and which job it belongs to. Location data is worth having too. Ideally the app captures photos in-app rather than letting people upload old shots from the camera roll.

3. Automatic upload that handles bad signal. Plenty of installs are on rural roofs with one bar of reception. The app should queue photos offline and upload them when the phone reconnects, without the installer babysitting it. Ask every vendor how this works and what happens when an upload fails.

4. Original-quality images. Serial labels and compliance stickers need to be readable. If the app compresses aggressively, you’re back to blurry evidence.

5. A photo checklist per job type. A rooftop PV install, a battery retrofit and an inverter swap need different shots. The app should prompt the crew through the list so nothing is missed, and the office should see at a glance what’s still outstanding.

6. Compliance sign-off. Somewhere for the installer to confirm the job is complete, plus the customer sign-off where you need it, stored against the job.

7. A proper sales-to-field handover. System design, site access notes, roof and switchboard details, the customer’s expectations, anything the rep promised. The crew should see it before they leave the yard, not dig for it in a thread.

8. Job-specific communication. Messages about 14 Smith Street belong on the 14 Smith Street job. That single change kills most of the noise in a group chat.

9. Subbie-friendly access and pricing. This is where a lot of tools fall over for solar. Many job management products price per user, and pricing pages like ServiceM8’s (ServiceM8, n.d.) show how plans are tiered. Check whether each subcontractor needs their own paid seat, whether there’s a cheaper field-only login, and whether access can be switched off the day a subbie finishes with you. If adding a crew costs you a full licence each time, you’ll be tempted to share logins, and shared logins destroy your audit trail.

10. Works on the phones they already own. Android and iPhone, older models included. I’ve written separately about choosing Android-friendly solar field software, because a lot of crews aren’t on the latest iPhone.

Categories of tools, and where they fit

I’m not going to rank brands. Products and pricing change too fast, and I won’t make feature claims about someone else’s software. What I can do is sort the options into categories.

Generic team chat (WhatsApp, Messenger, SMS). Free, fast, everyone already uses it. It has no job structure, no evidence trail and no control over the data. It’s fine for “running ten minutes late” and not much else.

Standalone photo capture apps. These are built for job photos: timestamped capture, cloud upload, sometimes checklists. They fix the evidence problem quickly. The trade-off is that they’re another login, another silo and another place the office has to check.

General field service management software. Scheduling, dispatch, job cards, invoicing. These are strong on running the day. They’re usually built for service trades, so look hard at how they handle install photo checklists, STC paperwork and subcontractor access.

Solar-specific platforms. These are built around the install workflow, which tends to mean better compliance and handover features. Check how well they suit Australian rules, since some are designed for overseas markets first.

The real problem for most businesses isn’t picking one of these. It’s that they end up with three of them, plus the chat group, and nobody’s sure which one holds the truth. I’ve called this the three-tool problem, and it’s worth reading before you add another app to the pile.

When WhatsApp plus some structure is enough

Let me be straight about this. Not every business needs new software tomorrow.

If you’re a small outfit doing a few installs a week with one trusted crew, you can get a long way with discipline:

  • One group per job, named with the address and install date, archived once the job is closed off
  • A strict naming convention for photos once they’re saved to a shared business drive
  • A written photo checklist the crew works through before leaving site
  • Send photos as documents/files rather than images where the app allows it, to avoid compression
  • The office downloads everything to business storage the same day, so the phone isn’t the record

That’s free and it’s a real improvement. But it relies on people doing it perfectly every time. Once you’ve got multiple crews, subbies coming and going, a sales team handing over jobs, or a claim that’s been queried, the manual version starts leaking. If you’re spending hours a week chasing photos, or you’ve ever been unable to find evidence for a job, it’s time.

A migration plan that won’t start a mutiny

The fastest way to fail is to announce on Monday that the group chat is dead and everyone must use the new app. The crews will keep using the chat, the app will sit empty, and you’ll have paid for nothing. Here’s the approach I’d take.

Step 1: Pick one crew. Ideally your most reliable one, with at least one person who’s comfortable on a phone. Don’t start with the subbie who still prints his invoices.

Step 2: Move photos first. Photos are where the chat fails worst and where the app gives the most obvious win. Tell the crew: keep chatting as normal, but every install photo goes in the app against the job.

Step 3: Move the handover next. Once the crew is used to opening the job in the app for photos, put the sales handover there too. They’ll find the job details are clearer than a forwarded screenshot of a quote.

Step 4: Run both in parallel for a short, fixed period. A few weeks is usually plenty. Set the end date up front so it doesn’t drag on forever.

Step 5: Retire the chat for job-critical information. Not for everything. People can still message “stuck in traffic”. But anything that’s evidence, a decision or a job detail lives in the app. If it’s not in the app, it didn’t happen.

Step 6: Roll out to the next crew, using the first crew as the people who show them how it works. Installers trust other installers far more than they trust the office.

Getting subcontractor buy-in

Subbies aren’t your employees, and they’re often working for two or three businesses at once. You can’t just order them onto your system. You have to make it worth their while. My broader thinking on this is in solar subcontractor management, but for apps specifically it comes down to three things.

Keep it on their own phone, and keep it clean. They don’t want your customers’ photos in their camera roll any more than you do. An app that captures and uploads in-app, without filling their personal gallery, is a selling point for them. Pair it with a clear BYOD policy so everyone knows the rules.

Make it faster than the chat. If the app takes more taps than sending a WhatsApp photo, they’ll go back to WhatsApp. Test it yourself on a roof-height ladder with gloves on, metaphorically at least.

Don’t add unpaid admin. Subbies are paid by the job. Every ten minutes of data entry on site is ten minutes they’re not being paid for. Give them a short checklist, not a form. And the biggest carrot of all: if the app makes their completion photos and sign-off the trigger for invoicing, so they get paid faster, buy-in sorts itself out.

FAQ

Can I use WhatsApp photos as STC evidence?

I wouldn’t rely on it. Chat apps can compress images, photos aren’t tied to a specific job, and it’s hard to prove when and where a shot was taken. Check the Clean Energy Regulator’s guidance for what’s required, and capture evidence in a way that keeps the original image and its details.

Do subcontractors need their own login to a field service app?

In most cases, yes. Shared logins wreck your audit trail because you can’t tell who took which photo or signed off which job. The real question is cost, so check whether the software offers a cheaper field-only seat and lets you switch off access the day a subbie finishes.

How long should I run the app and the group chat side by side?

Keep it short and set the end date up front. A few weeks is usually enough for one crew to get comfortable. After that, the chat stays for casual messages only and anything job-critical goes in the app.

Is a field service app worth it for a small solar business?

Not always straight away. If you’re running one crew and a few installs a week, a disciplined setup (one group per job, a photo checklist, same-day downloads to business storage) can do the job. Once you’ve got multiple crews, subbies coming and going, or you’ve lost evidence for a job, the structure pays for itself.

What’s the most important feature for solar crews?

Photo capture tied to the job, with reliable timestamps and automatic upload that works on poor signal. If that part is clumsy, the crew will go back to the chat, and nothing else the app does will matter.
