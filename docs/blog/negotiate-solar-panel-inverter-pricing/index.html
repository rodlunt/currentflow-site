Negotiating Solar Panel and Inverter Pricing With Distributors

By Rodney Lunt · 9 November 2026 · 13 min read

Share:

Most small installers treat the distributor price list as fixed. You get a sheet, you pick the kit, you pay the number. Then you hear that the bigger operator down the road is paying less for the same panel, and you assume it’s purely about volume and there’s nothing you can do.

I spent years on the supplier side of that conversation, representing solar and electrical specialist suppliers to installers of every size. I can tell you the price list is where the negotiation starts. The installers who did best with me weren’t always the biggest. They knew what to ask for, they knew what I actually valued, and they didn’t fixate on the per-watt number.

This is the playbook I wish more of them had used.

Why the usual advice doesn’t help you

Search for anything about negotiating solar panel and inverter pricing in Australia and you’ll get homeowner content. Quote-comparison guides tell buyers how to get three quotes and pick a brand (Your Solar Quotes, n.d.). State-by-state incentive guides walk through what a household can claim (Blooming Rays, n.d.). Installer explainers cover system costs, sizing and the 120% rule for the customer’s benefit (PS Electrical, n.d.).

That’s all useful for your customers. It’s useless for you. None of it says how a distributor structures volume tiers, what a small installer can trade for a better deal, or what to put in writing besides the price.

A quick word on terms before we go further. When a distributor talks about a “volume rebate”, they mean a commercial discount paid back to you for hitting a purchase target. That has nothing to do with STCs, which aren’t a rebate at all. They’re tradeable certificates under the Renewable Energy Target. If you need to explain that to your sales team, I’ve written it up in why STCs are not a rebate.

How distributor volume rebates and tiers typically work

Every distributor runs their programme differently, and I’m not going to quote anyone’s actual rates. The mechanics tend to fall into a handful of patterns, though, and you need to know which one you’re looking at before you can negotiate it.

The measurement period. Tiers are usually measured quarterly or annually. Quarterly is easier for a small business to hit and track. Annual tiers look generous on paper, but they let a distributor keep your money for twelve months and leave you exposed if your volume dips in winter.

Retrospective versus forward-looking. This one matters more than anything else on the sheet.

  • A retrospective rebate applies the better rate to all your volume in the period once you cross the threshold. Cross the line and every watt you bought gets cheaper.
  • A forward-looking (or incremental) rebate only applies the better rate to volume above the threshold, or only from the point you cross it.

The same headline discount can be worth several times more under one structure than the other. Always ask which it is, and get the answer in writing.

What counts toward the tier. Ask whether panels, inverters, batteries and balance-of-system all count, or only certain brands. Some programmes only count a preferred brand, which quietly locks your product choice. Ask whether returns and credit notes come off your volume and whether orders placed but not yet invoiced count.

How it’s paid. Rebates might come as a credit note against future purchases, a cash payment, or a reduced invoice price. Credit notes keep you buying from that distributor. Cash is worth more to you. You’ll also want to know when it’s paid. A rebate that lands sixty days after quarter end is a cashflow item you need to plan for, not money in the bank.

A worked example: when chasing a tier stops paying

Every number in this section is illustrative. They aren’t real distributor prices and they aren’t benchmarks. They’re there to show the maths.

Say your distributor offers this panel programme:

Quarterly volumeIllustrative price per watt
Under 200 kW$0.30
200 kW and over$0.28

You normally buy around 160 kW a quarter. The rep suggests you order an extra 40 kW before quarter end to reach the tier.

If the rebate is retrospective: all 200 kW gets the 2c/W reduction. That’s 200,000 W × $0.02 = $4,000 back. The extra 40 kW costs you 40,000 W × $0.28 = $11,200 in cash up front.

If the rebate is forward-looking: only the volume above the threshold gets the better rate. Hitting exactly 200 kW leaves almost nothing above the line, so the saving is close to zero. You’ve just tied up $11,200 in stock for no real reason.

Even in the retrospective case, the $4,000 isn’t all profit. Count the costs:

  • Carrying cost. If that $11,200 sits on the shelf for three months while you’re running an overdraft, you’re paying interest on it. At an illustrative 10% a year, that’s roughly $280.
  • Obsolescence. Panel wattages keep climbing. I’ve watched them go from 165W to over 600W in my time in this industry. Buy a model that gets superseded while it sits in your shed and you’ll either discount it to move it or quote an older panel against competitors offering the new one.
  • Damage and storage. Pallets get knocked, sheds get wet, stock goes missing.
  • Opportunity cost. That cash could have covered a supplier payment, wages, or the gap while you wait on certificate payments. If your money’s already stretched across that gap, I’ve covered it in the STC lodgement cashflow gap.

My rule from the other side of the counter: if you can’t see where the extra stock is going on your forward schedule (signed jobs, deposits paid), the tier isn’t a saving. It’s a bet on future sales funded with today’s cash. Cash timing kills more install businesses than thin margins do, and plenty of the cashflow advice aimed at installers comes back to the gap between profit on paper and cash in the account (SurgePV, n.d.).

What leverage a small installer actually has

You won’t out-volume the national players, and you don’t need to. In my experience, distributor reps care about more than raw kilowatts. Here’s what gets their attention.

Consistent monthly volume. A steady 40 kW every month is often worth more to a distributor’s planning than 200 kW once and then nothing for a quarter. Show your order history and point out that it’s predictable.

Fast, reliable payment. This is the most underrated leverage in the trade. Distributors carry real credit risk across hundreds of small accounts, and an account that pays on time, every time, costs them less to serve. If you can pay early, offer it in exchange for a price or settlement discount. Just be honest with yourself about whether your cashflow can hold that promise.

Forecasting. Give your rep a rolling forecast of what you expect to need over the next eight to twelve weeks. They can plan stock, keep your preferred lines in, and argue for better pricing internally because you’re a lower-risk account. Very few small installers do this, which makes you stand out when you do.

Brand flexibility. If you’re willing to move onto a range the distributor wants to push, that’s worth something to them. Trade it deliberately, though, and only for brands you’d actually stand behind. A cheap panel from a manufacturer that won’t be here to honour the warranty costs you more than it ever saved. I’ve written about that risk in solar brand longevity and warranty risk.

Buying groups and shared purchasing. Some installers pool volume through a buying group or an informal arrangement with other operators. That can unlock tiers you’d never reach alone. Before you sign up, work out who holds the account, who carries the credit risk, how the rebate gets split, and what happens if a member stops paying.

A credible second supplier. You negotiate better when you can genuinely walk away. That doesn’t mean threatening your rep every week. It means having a second qualified account so you aren’t hostage to one price list. I’ve covered how to set that up in why you shouldn’t rely on one solar supplier.

What to negotiate beyond the headline price

The per-watt price is the number everyone fights over. The terms around it usually decide whether you make money.

Payment terms and credit limits

Thirty days versus sixty days, the credit limit, and whether there’s a settlement discount for early payment can all be worth more than a cent or two per watt. Get your terms of trade in writing and read them properly, including the retention of title clause, interest on overdue accounts, and what lets the distributor put you on stop. Legal explainers on terms of trade for renewable energy businesses are a good primer on what these documents usually contain (Sprintlaw, n.d.). For how title works when stock isn’t paid for, see retention of title clauses on solar panels.

Stock availability and lead times

A cheap price on stock that isn’t there is worth nothing. Ask which lines are held locally, typical lead times, how backorders are communicated, and whether they’ll allocate stock to you during a shortage. Supply constraints on inverters have come up before; manufacturers have publicly flagged global chip and raw material pressure as a serious problem for the sector (SMA Australia, n.d.). Ask how your distributor handled the last squeeze and who got stock first.

Compliant, current product

Make sure the range you’re committing volume to is currently CEC-listed and meets current inverter requirements. Rule changes around inverter compliance and dynamic export settings mean older stock can turn into a problem (Daily Energy News, n.d.). Ask the distributor outright what happens to stock that falls off the approved list while it’s still on your shelf.

Warranty and defect handling

When a panel or inverter fails, your customer’s rights sit with you under the Australian Consumer Law, which covers repair, replacement and refund for faulty goods (ACCC, n.d.). Your rights against the distributor depend on what you negotiated. Pin down who pays for the labour on a warranty swap, how fast a replacement ships, whether you get advance replacement or have to wait for an assessment, and who deals with the manufacturer. My checklist for this is in vetting the solar equipment warranty process.

Returns and credit notes

Get the returns policy in writing: restocking fees, the window for returning unopened stock, how long a credit note takes to issue, and whether it comes off your rebate volume. Most installers only find out the rules on their first big return.

Negotiation scripts you can adapt

None of this needs to be confrontational. Reps do better when they can take a clear, reasonable case to their manager. Give them one.

Opening the conversation:

“I’ve been buying steadily with you for a while and I want to grow that. Before I commit more volume, I want to understand the rebate structure properly. Is it retrospective or forward-looking, and what counts toward the tier?”

Trading payment for price:

“My account’s always paid on time. If I moved to paying in fourteen days instead of thirty, what would that do to my pricing or settlement discount?”

Using a forecast:

“Here’s my forecast for the next twelve weeks. If I can give you that every month, can you hold stock of these lines for me and look at my tier pricing?”

Pushing back on a stock-up offer:

“I’d rather not buy ahead of my pipeline. Could you count my average monthly volume toward the tier instead, or split the threshold across two quarters?”

Locking in warranty terms:

“When one of these inverters fails, how does it work in practice? Who covers the labour, how fast does the replacement ship, and can you put that in writing?”

Pre-call checklist

Have this ready before you pick up the phone:

  1. Your purchase history by month for the past twelve months.
  2. A forecast based on signed and deposited jobs, not hope.
  3. Your current terms of trade, printed out and marked up.
  4. The comparison table filled in for at least one alternative supplier.
  5. Your actual job margins, so you know what a cent per watt is worth to you.
  6. A clear list of the three things you want most, ranked.
  7. The point where you’ll walk away.

Point five is where most installers come unstuck. Benchmarks only give you a rough guide. One industry source puts gross margins for installers roughly in the 20 to 35 per cent range (Why Solar, n.d.), and the ATO publishes general small business benchmarks you can compare against (Australian Taxation Office, n.d.). Neither tells you what your jobs make. Proper job costing does.

Make sure the deal survives the job

A better distributor deal only counts if it shows up in your margin. Plenty of installers negotiate a sharper price and then lose the gain to a warranty callout nobody costed, a pallet nobody tracked, or a rebate credit nobody claimed.

FAQ

Can a small solar installer really negotiate with a distributor?

Yes. You won’t win on volume against the national players, but you can trade consistent monthly orders, fast payment, accurate forecasts and some brand flexibility. Distributors value predictable, low-risk accounts, and a small installer who’s organised is often easier to deal with than a big one who isn’t.

What’s the difference between a retrospective and a forward-looking volume rebate?

A retrospective rebate applies the better rate to all your volume in the period once you cross the threshold. A forward-looking rebate only applies it to volume above the threshold. The same headline discount can be worth very different amounts depending on which one you’ve got, so always ask and get it in writing.

Should I order extra stock to hit a rebate tier?

Only if that stock is already earmarked for signed, deposited jobs on your schedule. Otherwise you’re tying up cash, taking on storage and obsolescence risk, and betting on sales you haven’t made. Run the numbers on carrying costs and the rebate structure before you agree.

Is a distributor volume rebate the same as an STC?

No. A volume rebate is a commercial discount from your supplier. STCs are tradeable certificates created under the Renewable Energy Target, and they aren’t a rebate of any kind. Keep the two separate in your books and in how you talk to customers.

What matters more than the per-watt price?

Payment terms, credit limits, local stock availability, lead times, warranty swap handling, returns policy and whether the range is currently compliant and CEC-listed. A slightly higher price with solid terms can easily beat a cheaper price that comes with stockouts and messy warranty claims.
