AI Chatbot or Live Chat for Solar Warranty Enquiries?

By Rodney Lunt · 22 October 2026 · 12 min read

Share:

A customer messages your website at 9pm on a Sunday: “My inverter is showing a red light and the app says zero generation. Is this covered?” What happens next decides whether you keep a happy customer or pick up a one-star review and a Fair Trading complaint.

Most solar businesses I talk to have no plan for that message. It lands in a generic inbox, someone sees it Monday afternoon, and by then the customer has already rung the manufacturer, been told to “contact your installer”, and decided you’re dodging them.

The vendors are now pitching AI chatbots as the fix. Some of that pitch is fair. A lot of it skips the part that actually makes solar warranty enquiries hard. This article is the comparison I’d want if I were choosing today.

What the vendor pages don’t tell you

Search for chatbots for solar warranty enquiries and you’ll find a handful of vendor pages. Convocore (n.d.) has a page aimed at warranty claims for solar installers, Flowmatic AI (2026) publishes a buyer’s guide for solar companies, and Yep AI (n.d.) markets chatbots for solar businesses.

They are marketing pages, and that’s fine; they’re selling. But read them closely and most of the emphasis sits on capturing and qualifying leads, booking consultations and answering pre-sale questions. Warranty support gets treated as one more thing a bot can answer, as if it were just another FAQ.

It isn’t. Warranty enquiries are where your legal obligations, your supplier relationships and your reputation all collide in one conversation. A bot that’s brilliant at qualifying leads can do real damage if it gives a customer the wrong answer about who is responsible for a fault.

Start with the question every warranty enquiry hides

Before you choose any tool, get clear on this: every warranty enquiry is really one of three things, and the customer usually doesn’t know which.

  1. A manufacturer product warranty issue. The panel, inverter or battery itself has failed. The product warranty sits with the manufacturer, usually actioned through the supplier and, in practice, through you.
  2. An installer workmanship issue. A roof leak at a penetration, a loose isolator, a DC connector that wasn’t mated properly. That’s yours.
  3. A consumer guarantee issue under the Australian Consumer Law. This sits underneath both of the above, regardless of what your written warranty says.

The federal government’s consumer guidance on solar warranties draws the same line between product warranties, workmanship warranties and the statutory rights that sit alongside them (Australian Government, n.d.). Solar Victoria’s guidance breaks warranties down the same way (Solar Victoria, n.d.).

The ACL piece is the one that catches businesses out. The ACCC is clear that consumers can be entitled to a repair, replacement or refund when goods or services fail to meet the consumer guarantees, and those rights exist alongside any warranty a business offers (ACCC, n.d.). So “that’s a manufacturer problem, ring them” isn’t a safe answer from you, and it’s definitely not a safe answer from a bot.

If you want the detail on vetting the product side before it ever becomes a claim, I’ve covered that in how to vet a solar equipment warranty process. If a manufacturer exits the market, the problem lands on your desk even faster, which is what solar brand longevity and warranty risk is about.

The practical point: whatever sits on your website has to triage before it answers. Its first job is to collect enough information to work out which bucket the enquiry belongs in. Deciding who is at fault comes later.

Where an AI chatbot genuinely earns its keep

I’m not anti-bot. I’ve implemented enough CRMs and written enough follow-up playbooks to know that the majority of inbound questions are the same dozen questions asked over and over. Automation is good at that.

An AI chatbot suits warranty enquiries when the job is:

  • Answering repeatable questions. “How long is my panel warranty?” “What does the workmanship warranty cover?” “How do I lodge a claim?” These have answers you’ve already written down, or should have.
  • Collecting the triage information. Install address, approximate install date, inverter brand, what the monitoring app is showing, photos of the inverter display or any visible damage. A bot can ask for these patiently at 9pm without anyone getting tired.
  • Holding the customer after hours. A clear “we’ve logged this, here’s your reference, a person will call you by [time] tomorrow” is worth more than silence.
  • Walking through basic checks. Is the inverter on? Has the app lost wi-fi? A lot of “my system is dead” enquiries turn out to be a monitoring dropout. A scripted check list can sort some of those before a truck rolls.

That last one alone can save your field team a wasted trip. A callback that turns out to be a router change is pure lost margin.

Where you need a human, full stop

There are warranty conversations a bot should never try to finish. Build your setup so these get handed to a person immediately:

  • Anything that sounds like a safety issue. Burning smell, scorching, water near the inverter or isolator, smoke, a tripped main that keeps tripping. The bot’s only job here is to give your standard safety instruction (stay away, isolate if safe, call you or emergency services) and escalate. It shouldn’t troubleshoot.
  • Disputed faults. If the customer thinks it’s workmanship and you suspect a product failure, or the reverse, that’s a judgement call with legal weight. A bot “deciding” in a transcript is evidence you don’t want.
  • Upset customers. Someone who has already been bounced between you and the manufacturer doesn’t want a cheerful bot. They want a person who owns it.
  • Anything that touches a refund, replacement or ACL remedy. Promises made in writing are promises. Keep those with someone who has authority to make them.

The failure I’ve watched repeat itself is businesses buying a tool to cut workload and letting it answer questions it was never qualified to answer. The workload drops for a month, then a dispute blows up and the transcript shows the bot told the customer something you’d never have said.

Live chat: better conversations, harder to staff

Live chat solves the judgement problem. A real person can read tone, recognise a safety risk and make a sensible call on workmanship versus product. The problem is coverage.

Be honest about your team. In a small install business, the person who’d answer live chat is usually also doing quotes, chasing STC paperwork, booking subbies and answering the phone. Live chat that shows “online” and then takes forty minutes to respond is worse than a form, because you’ve set an expectation and broken it.

Customers don’t expect a warranty fault fixed within the hour. They do expect to know their message was received, who is handling it and when they’ll hear back. That’s the response standard to design for: acknowledgement fast, human contact within a business day, and a clear next step. It’s the same principle behind instant enquiry responses for new leads, just applied to existing customers, who are frankly worth more to you.

Record-keeping: the part nobody budgets for

Here’s what the chatbot vendors rarely lead with. Every warranty conversation is a record. If it ends up as a dispute, a tribunal matter or a complaint, the transcript is evidence of what the customer reported, when they reported it and what you told them.

On the compliance side, the Clean Energy Regulator expects installers and designers to keep proper evidence of installations, including the photos and documentation that support STC creation (Clean Energy Regulator, n.d.). (STCs, for any newer operators, are tradeable certificates created from an eligible system and sold to liable entities, not a rebate.) A warranty claim often sends you straight back to those install records: serial numbers, commissioning results, photos of the original work.

So your warranty channel needs to do three things with every conversation:

  1. Keep the transcript, retrievable later, not locked inside a chat vendor you might cancel next year.
  2. Attach it to the original job record, so whoever picks it up can see the install date, equipment serials, installer and commissioning sheet in one place.
  3. Log what happened next: call made, site visit booked, claim lodged with the supplier, outcome.

Check the data export and retention terms on any chat tool before you sign. If you can’t get your transcripts out in a usable format, you don’t really own your own records.

The integration gap that creates double handling

This is where most setups fall over. The chat tool captures the enquiry. Then someone copies it into an email, then into the job system, then into a spreadsheet of open warranty claims, then into an email to the supplier. Same information, typed four times, with a mistake waiting at every step.

Generic help desk platforms are built around tickets for the very reason that untracked conversations get lost (Zendesk, n.d.). But a generic ticket isn’t enough in solar. The ticket needs to know which install it relates to, what equipment went on the roof and who installed it. Without that, your team spends the first ten minutes of every warranty call hunting through old folders.

If that sounds familiar, it’s the same disease I wrote about in the three-tool problem in solar software. Bolting a chatbot onto disconnected systems just adds a fourth tool.

Once a claim becomes a site visit, it should flow into a proper rectification job, with an owner, a date and a close-out. I’ve covered that workflow in managing solar defect rectification jobs.

The real cost tradeoff

Chatbot vendors usually sell on a monthly subscription. Live chat is cheaper as software but expensive in time, because it needs a person watching it. I won’t quote figures here because pricing moves constantly and depends on volume, but the shape of the decision is consistent:

  • Software subscriptions are predictable. You know what you’ll pay each month. The hidden cost is setup: writing accurate answers, defining escalation rules and testing it properly.
  • Live chat staffing costs are lumpy. Either someone is dedicated to it (a real wage) or it’s tacked onto an existing role (and quotes slow down instead).
  • The biggest cost is the one you don’t see: a badly handled warranty enquiry that becomes a complaint, a chargeback, a bad review or a lost referral. One of those can cost more than a year of software.

A simple decision framework by business size

Owner-operator or very small team. Skip live chat. Use a well-structured warranty enquiry form or a narrowly scoped bot that triages, collects details and photos, gives safety instructions, and promises a callback within a set time. Then actually call back.

Growing business with an office person or two. A hybrid works best. A bot handles after-hours triage and the repeatable questions; live chat switches on during office hours when someone is genuinely available. Everything lands as a ticket against the job.

Larger retailer with a dedicated service team. Live chat with a bot front-end makes sense, provided it’s integrated with your job and CRM records. At this size, an unintegrated chat tool is costing you real hours every week.

Whatever the size, write the escalation rules first and choose the tool second. If you can’t write down exactly when a human takes over, no product will fix that for you.

Where I’m landing on this

The tool matters less than the record behind it. A warranty enquiry is only handled well if it can be matched to the original install, triaged correctly and tracked to a close.

FAQ

Can an AI chatbot decide whether a fault is covered by warranty?

It shouldn’t. A chatbot can collect the details and explain what your written warranties generally cover, but deciding between a product fault, a workmanship fault and a consumer guarantee issue is a judgement call with legal weight. Have the bot triage and escalate, and leave the decision to a person.

Is live chat better than a chatbot for solar warranty enquiries?

Live chat gives better conversations, but only if someone is genuinely available to answer quickly. For most small install businesses, a chatbot for after-hours triage plus a guaranteed human callback is more reliable than live chat that sits unattended.

Should I keep chat transcripts from warranty enquiries?

Yes. Treat them as part of the job record. They show what the customer reported and what you told them, which matters if a claim is disputed. Make sure your chat tool lets you export and keep transcripts, and attach them to the original install record.

What should a chatbot do if a customer reports a safety issue?

Give your standard safety instruction straight away (stay clear, isolate only if safe, contact emergency services if there’s fire or smoke) and escalate to a person immediately. It should not try to troubleshoot a potential safety fault.

Can I just tell customers to contact the manufacturer for product faults?

Be careful with that. The customer’s consumer guarantee rights can sit with the business that sold them the system, alongside the manufacturer’s warranty. Telling customers to “ring the manufacturer” as a blanket answer is a common way to turn a fixable fault into a complaint.
