Pay Solar Installers When STC or Finance Money Is Late

By Rodney Lunt · 11 November 2026 · 14 min read

Share:

The job’s done. The panels are on the roof, the inverter’s commissioned and your installer has sent their invoice. But the STC money hasn’t come through. The finance provider hasn’t settled either, and the customer’s balance is stuck behind both of them. Your subbie still has a mortgage, a ute payment and a crew to pay on Thursday.

Search for help with this and nearly everything you find is written for the subcontractor chasing money: lien rights, demand letters, US construction stats. Very little is written for the person in the middle, the solar business owner who wants to pay their installers properly but is waiting on money that hasn’t landed yet.

This article is for that person. I’ve traded STCs, sat on the supplier side of the counter and watched install businesses run the same cash-flow race for twenty years. The businesses that keep good installers aren’t the ones that never get squeezed. They’re the ones that planned for the squeeze and told their crews the rules before the job started.

General information only, not legal or financial advice. Get a lawyer and an accountant to look at your own contracts.

The payer’s problem, named plainly

Most solar install businesses carry a timing gap on every job. You pay out for labour, panels, inverters and sometimes cranage or scaffolding up front, and the money comes back from three or four places at different speeds:

  • The customer deposit, usually paid at contract, often small.
  • STC proceeds, which arrive once the paperwork is lodged, validated and paid out by your agent or trader.
  • Finance or BNPL settlement, which usually depends on the customer signing off on completion.
  • The customer balance, if there is one.

Your subcontractor’s invoice usually lands before most of that money does. If you’ve never written down when you’ll pay them, they’ll assume “now”, and they’re entitled to.

I’ve written before about the gap between install and STC payment, and how sloppy lodgement timing makes it worse. This piece is about what you do when that gap exists anyway, and how to keep your installers paid and loyal through it.

Why STC money is late (and why it’s not a rebate delay)

First, a correction I’ll keep making until the industry stops getting it wrong. STCs are not a rebate. STCs are not a rebate explains it in full, but the short version is this: Small-scale Technology Certificates are tradeable certificates created from an eligible system’s deemed generation. Electricity retailers with obligations under the Renewable Energy Target have to buy them and surrender them to the Clean Energy Regulator (n.d.-a).

Your customer normally assigns their right to create the certificates to you, and you discount the price. Then you either sell them yourself, sell through an agent or trader on the open market, or offer them through the STC Clearing House at a fixed price. The Clearing House can take a long time to pay because it works as a queue (Clean Energy Regulator, n.d.-b).

Traders who buy from installers point out that payment time depends heavily on their own terms, their compliance checks and how fast the registry processes the certificates (Energy Merchants, n.d.-a). A missing signature, a mismatched serial number or a photo that doesn’t pass review can push a job out by weeks. That’s not the government being slow. That’s paperwork.

The other point that matters to cash flow: the STC price moves with the market. If you’re holding certificates waiting for a better price, or waiting because the paperwork bounced, you’re also carrying price risk on money you’ve effectively promised your installer.

Option 1: Get the STC money faster

The cleanest bridge is not to have a gap in the first place.

Use a trader or agent with fast, clear payment terms. Some agents pay quickly once the paperwork passes their checks; others pay on a set schedule. Expect to give up some value for speed. A faster payer may take a bigger margin or offer a lower price per certificate (Energy Merchants, n.d.-b).

When you compare traders, ask:

  • How many business days from submission to payment, and what does the clock start from?
  • What happens if a certificate fails registry validation, and is there a clawback?
  • Is the price locked when you submit or when they pay?
  • What fees sit on top of the quoted price?

Fix the paperwork upstream. In my experience, more STC delays come from incomplete job packs than from anything the trader or regulator does. If your installer can’t leave the site until the photos, serials and signatures are captured, you take a lot of the delay out of the cycle. Your installer has a stake in that too, so tell them.

Trade-off: you give up some certificate value in exchange for cash sooner. If that discount is smaller than what a late payment costs you in installer goodwill, it’s worth it.

Option 2: Bridge with finance

When the gap is structural, because you’re growing and every month carries more jobs in flight, you may need outside working capital. The common options are:

  • Invoice finance or factoring. A lender advances a portion of the value of receivables (finance settlements, commercial invoices) and takes a fee. It works best where the debtor is creditworthy and the invoice is clean.
  • Short-term unsecured working capital. Fast to arrange, usually expensive. Fine for a known, short gap. Dangerous as a habit.
  • A business overdraft or line of credit. Cheaper if you can get one, but banks will want to see your numbers.

Solar-focused cash-flow writers make the same core point: profit and cash are not the same thing, and a business can be profitable on paper while it can’t make payroll (SurgePV, 2026). Finance is a tool for covering timing, not for covering a margin problem. If your jobs don’t make money, borrowing to pay installers only delays the reckoning, and as a director you need to keep insolvent trading risk front of mind.

Trade-off: fees and interest come straight off your margin. Work out the cost per job before you sign anything, and compare it to the cost of a faster STC payout.

Option 3: Restructure your inflows

This is the lever most install businesses underuse. If more cash comes in before and during the install, the gap at the end shrinks.

Deposits. Take a deposit that covers a meaningful share of your equipment cost, within whatever limits apply to your work and state. Queensland, for example, has statutory rules on deposits and progress payments for regulated building work (Queensland Building and Construction Commission [QBCC], n.d.-a). Check whether your jobs are caught before you set a figure. Consumer law also has a say in how deposits are handled; see solar deposits and Australian consumer rights.

Staged or progress payments. For larger residential and commercial jobs, split the price into milestones: deposit, equipment delivery, install complete, commissioning. Payment terms for solar installation contracts should spell out what triggers each instalment and what happens if it isn’t paid (Sprintlaw, n.d.-a). I’ve covered the mechanics in milestone payment billing for solar installers.

Finance and BNPL. Know exactly what each provider needs before it settles, and get your customer’s completion sign-off on the day of install, not a week later. The providers differ more than most people realise; comparing Brighte, Plenti and humm is a good starting point.

Write your subbie payment terms before the job, not after

This is where loyalty is won or lost. In my experience, installers rarely leave over a payment arriving a week later than they’d hoped. They leave when it’s late without warning, and when they don’t know where they stand.

Process guides for subcontractor payments keep landing on the same fundamentals: clear contract terms, defined payment triggers, consistent documentation and prompt communication (AIA Contract Documents, n.d.). Even the payment-platform vendors make the point that predictable, on-time payment is what keeps good subbies coming back (Checkbook, n.d.).

So write it into the subcontractor agreement up front:

  • What counts as complete. For example: install done, commissioning done, all compliance documents and job photos submitted and accepted.
  • When payment is due. A set number of days after verified completion. Pick a number you can actually meet.
  • Whether part of the payment is staged. Covered below.
  • How variations are approved and paid.
  • How they invoice you. Tax invoice, or recipient-created tax invoice (RCTI).

If you’re paying per job rather than by day rate, paying solar installers per job covers how to set those rates so the terms hold up.

The legal catch: “we haven’t been paid yet” isn’t a defence

Here’s where many owners get caught out. Every state and territory has security of payment legislation. It gives people who carry out construction work a statutory right to make payment claims and to get those claims adjudicated quickly (QBCC, n.d.-b).

Whether a particular solar install counts as “construction work” under your state’s Act is a question for your lawyer. Electrical work attached to a building often will. If it does, a few things follow.

Pay-when-paid clauses need real care. Security of payment laws generally make clauses that tie your payment to you being paid by someone else unenforceable. Writing “payment within 7 days of STC settlement” into the agreement may not protect you the way you think. Get advice before you rely on settlement-linked terms. A trigger tied to verified completion is usually safer.

Valid payment claims need a timely response. If an installer serves a valid payment claim and you dispute it, you generally have to respond with a payment schedule within the statutory timeframe, setting out what you’ll pay and why. If you don’t respond, you can lose the right to dispute the amount (Boss Lawyers, n.d.). The timeframes differ by state and change over time, so check the current Act rather than trusting a figure in a blog post.

Your installer has real tools. Claim-filing services exist specifically to help subcontractors write valid payment claims (Payclaim, n.d.). Generic advice to unpaid subbies tells them to document everything and escalate formally (Contract Doctors, n.d.). A subbie who feels strung along will use those tools, and they’d be right to.

The practical takeaway: plan your cash so you can pay on agreed completion terms, and treat STC and finance timing as your problem, not theirs.

Keep the invoicing clean: tax invoices, RCTIs, GST and ABNs

A messy invoice trail turns a timing problem into an audit problem. Basics:

Check the ABN on every new subbie. If a contractor doesn’t quote an ABN, you may have to withhold tax from the payment. Checking ABN registration takes two minutes. Also check whether they’re registered for GST before you pay GST on top.

Know what a valid tax invoice needs. GST-registered suppliers have to issue tax invoices that meet ATO requirements, and you need one to claim the GST credit.

Consider RCTIs if you pay a lot of subbies. A recipient-created tax invoice is one you, the payer, issue on the subcontractor’s behalf. It only works if both parties are registered for GST, there’s a written agreement in place, and the invoice meets the ATO’s requirements, including being clearly labelled as an RCTI (Australian Taxation Office [ATO], n.d.). Done properly, RCTIs mean you control the timing, the amount and the format, which makes reconciling against job milestones much easier (Sprintlaw, n.d.-b).

Report contractor payments. Businesses in building and construction generally have annual reporting obligations for payments to contractors. Confirm with your accountant how that applies to you.

A sample payment schedule

Here’s one structure I’d consider. Percentages are illustrative only. Set your own based on your margins and cash position, and get legal advice on how it sits against security of payment rules in your state.

StageTriggerPayment to installer
CompletionInstall and commissioning done, job pack (photos, serials, signatures) submitted and acceptedMajority of the agreed job rate, within an agreed number of days
Final balanceA fixed number of days after completion, on a set date that does not depend on STC or finance settlementRemaining balance
VariationsPre-approved in writingPaid with the completion payment

The point of the structure: the installer is paid most of the money on something within their control (finishing the job and the paperwork), and the balance on a known date. You carry the settlement risk, but you’ve planned for it.

A template message for when you’re running late

Sometimes the plan breaks anyway. When it does, tell your installer before the due date, not after. Something like:

G’day [name],

Heads up on the [suburb] job. Your invoice is due on [date] under our agreement. We’re waiting on [STC payment / finance settlement] which has been held up because [brief, honest reason].

I don’t want you carrying that, so here’s what I’ll do: [amount or portion] will be in your account by [date], and the balance by [date]. If anything changes, you’ll hear it from me first.

Thanks for your patience. Ring me if this causes you a problem and we’ll work it out.

Keep it short, honest and specific. A date you’ll actually meet beats a vague promise. And if you send this message often, the problem is your cash planning, not the STC agent.

Where I’m going with this

What strikes me after years of watching install businesses is that the payment problem is usually a visibility problem. The owner doesn’t know, at a glance, which jobs are complete, which are waiting on STCs, which are waiting on finance, and what each installer is owed. So the gap gets discovered when a subbie rings, not planned for weeks ahead.

That’s part of why I’m building CurrentFlow. It’s designed to link subcontractor payments to job milestones like verified completion and photo evidence, and to show each installer’s payment status against STC and finance settlement. The idea is that you’ll see the shortfall coming and plan for it, rather than finding out on payday.

Whatever tools you use, the rules are the same. Get the paperwork right on site. Collect more cash before and during the install. Put payment terms in writing before the job. Pay on completion, not on settlement. And when you’re late, say so first. For more on keeping your crews onside, see my guide to solar subcontractor management.

FAQ

Can I make my installer wait until the STC money comes through?

Only if they’ve agreed to it, and even then it may not hold up. Security of payment laws generally limit pay-when-paid clauses, and a valid payment claim needs a proper response within the statutory timeframe. It’s safer to tie payment to verified job completion and carry the STC timing risk yourself. Get legal advice for your state.

Is it worth selling STCs at a discount to get paid faster?

Often, yes. Work out what a late payment costs you, in finance fees and in installer goodwill, and compare it to the discount a faster trader charges. If the discount is smaller, take the speed. Check clawback terms and when the price is locked before you commit.

Should I use recipient-created tax invoices for my subbies?

RCTIs can make high-volume subcontractor payments cleaner and easier to reconcile. Both parties have to be registered for GST, you need a written agreement, and the invoices have to meet ATO requirements. Talk to your accountant before you switch.

What’s the best way to tell an installer their payment will be late?

Tell them before the due date, explain the reason honestly, and give a specific date for a part payment and the balance. Then meet those dates. Installers forgive a planned delay far more readily than a surprise one.

How do I stop the gap happening on every job?

Collect more cash before and during the install through deposits and staged payments. Get compliance paperwork right on site so STCs aren’t delayed. Get finance completion sign-off on install day. And have enough visibility over jobs in flight that you can see a shortfall weeks out.
